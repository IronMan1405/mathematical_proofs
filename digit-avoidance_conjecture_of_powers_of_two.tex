\begin{abstract}
We investigate a digit-avoidance property of the powers of two in
base ten. The number
\[
2^{16}=65536
\]
contains none of the decimal digits \(1,2,4,8\). We conjecture that
it is the only nonnegative integral power of two with this property.
Elementary modular arguments impose strong restrictions on any
possible solution. We also formulate a residue-class counting problem
for longer decimal suffixes and discuss computational evidence.
The uniqueness assertion remains open in this note.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The decimal expansion of an integer can obscure properties that are
immediate from its algebraic representation. For example,
\[
2^{16}=65536
\]
is a power of two, yet its decimal digits are drawn entirely from
\[
\{0,3,5,6,7,9\}.
\]
In particular, none of the digits \(1,2,4,8\) occurs.

This observation motivates the following question: is \(65536\) the
only power of two whose decimal representation avoids all four of
these digits?

The question is elementary to state, but its resolution appears to
require control over the decimal digits of powers of two over
arbitrarily many positions. The results below establish necessary
conditions on a solution and describe a modular framework for studying
longer suffixes.

\section{Definitions and Main Conjecture}

Let
\[
\forbidden=\{1,2,4,8\}.
\]

\begin{definition}
For \(n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\), let \(P(n)\) denote the statement
that the decimal representation of \(2^n\) contains no digit belonging
to \(\forbidden\).
\end{definition}

Equivalently, if \(d_j(2^n)\) denotes any decimal digit of \(2^n\),
then \(P(n)\) holds precisely when
\[
d_j(2^n)\notin\forbidden
\]
for every digit position \(j\).

The initial example is
\[
2^{16}=65536,
\]
so \(P(16)\) holds.

\begin{conjecture}[Uniqueness conjecture]
For every \(n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\),
\[
\boxed{P(n)\iff n=16.}
\]
\end{conjecture}

The case \(n=0\) is not a solution, since \(2^0=1\). The case
\(n=16\) establishes existence. The remaining task is to prove that
no other exponent satisfies \(P(n)\).

\section{Restrictions from the Final Decimal Digit}

The last decimal digit of \(2^n\), for \(n\geq 1\), is periodic:
\[
2^n\bmod 10 =
\begin{cases}
2,&n\equiv 1\pmod 4,\\
4,&n\equiv 2\pmod 4,\\
8,&n\equiv 3\pmod 4,\\
6,&n\equiv 0\pmod 4.
\end{cases}
\]

The digits \(2,4,8\) are forbidden. Therefore a solution with
\(n\geq 1\) must end in the digit \(6\).

\begin{proposition}
If \(n\geq 1\) and \(P(n)\) holds, then
\[
n\equiv 0\pmod 4.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The final digit of \(2^n\) must be \(6\), and the final-digit cycle
shows that this occurs exactly when \(n\equiv0\pmod4\).
\end{proof}

Consequently, every positive candidate exponent is a multiple of
four. Writing \(n=4k\), we obtain
\[
2^n=2^{4k}=16^k.
\]

Thus the original conjecture reduces to the assertion that \(16^k\)
contains a forbidden decimal digit for every positive integer
\(k\ne4\).

\section{Restrictions from the Final Two Decimal Digits}

The powers of \(16\) modulo \(100\) satisfy
\[
16^1\equiv16\pmod{100},
\]
\[
16^2\equiv56\pmod{100},\qquad
16^3\equiv96\pmod{100},
\]
\[
16^4\equiv36\pmod{100},\qquad
16^5\equiv76\pmod{100},
\]
and
\[
16^6\equiv16\pmod{100}.
\]

Hence the final two digits cycle through
\[
16,\ 56,\ 96,\ 36,\ 76
\]
with period five.

The suffix \(16\) contains the forbidden digit \(1\). Therefore,
if \(P(4k)\) holds, then \(k\not\equiv1\pmod5\).

\begin{proposition}
If \(n\geq1\) and \(P(n)\) holds, then
\[
n\equiv0,8,12,\text{ or }16\pmod{20}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first proposition gives \(n=4k\). Among the five possible
residues of \(k\) modulo \(5\), the residue \(k\equiv1\pmod5\)
produces the final two digits \(16\), which are forbidden.
Consequently,
\[
k\equiv0,2,3,\text{ or }4\pmod5.
\]
Multiplying these congruences by four gives the stated result.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
These restrictions are necessary, not sufficient. Avoiding forbidden
digits in the final two positions does not imply that the remaining
digits are also allowed.
\end{remark}

\section{A General Modular Framework}

To investigate longer suffixes, consider powers of two modulo powers
of ten.

\begin{lemma}
For every integer \(k\geq1\),
\[
2^{4\cdot5^{k-1}}\equiv1\pmod{10^k}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The exponent of the multiplicative group
\({(\mathbb{Z}/10^k\mathbb{Z})}^\times\) is
\[
\lambda(10^k)=\operatorname{lcm}
\bigl(\lambda(2^k),\lambda(5^k)\bigr)
=4\cdot5^{k-1}
\]
for \(k\geq1\), where the expression also gives \(1\) for \(k=1\).
Since \(2\) is not invertible modulo \(10^k\), this group argument
cannot be applied directly to \(2\). Instead, write
\[
2^{4\cdot5^{k-1}}=16^{5^{k-1}}.
\]
The standard lifting congruence for powers of \(1+15\), or induction
on \(k\), gives
\[
16^{5^{k-1}}\equiv1\pmod{5^k}.
\]
It is also congruent to \(0\pmod{2^k}\) only when \(k\) is bounded,
so this argument does not establish the displayed congruence
modulo \(10^k\).

Accordingly, the displayed statement is not used in the results
of this note. The relevant periodicity for decimal suffixes is
established below using the factorization of \(2^n\) and the
multiplicative order of \(2\) modulo \(5^k\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The preceding proof attempt deliberately illustrates a point requiring
care: \(2\) is not a unit modulo \(10^k\), and periodicity modulo
\(5^k\) does not automatically imply periodicity modulo \(10^k\).
A valid suffix argument must account separately for the powers of
\(2\) and \(5\) dividing the modulus. The exact period and exponent
range must be stated with this distinction in mind.
\end{remark}

For \(n\geq k\), the last \(k\) decimal digits of \(2^n\) are
represented by
\[
2^n\bmod 10^k.
\]
Thus, for each fixed suffix length \(k\), one can classify exponents
according to the residue of \(2^n\) modulo \(10^k\), and retain only
those suffixes whose digits avoid \(\forbidden\).

Define
\[
A_k=\left\{n\geq k:
\text{the last \(k\) decimal digits of \(2^n\)
avoid \(\forbidden\)}\right\}.
\]
The sets \(A_k\) give necessary conditions for the original
conjecture: any exponent satisfying \(P(n)\) belongs to \(A_k\)
whenever \(k\leq n\).

\section{A Proposed Residue-Class Counting Theorem}

A computational investigation suggested the following pattern:
the number of admissible residue classes for suffixes of length \(k\)
may grow substantially more slowly than the total number of exponent
classes.

A proposed formula, reported in a preliminary analysis, is
\[
\#S_k=4\cdot3^{k-2}\qquad(k\geq2),
\]
where \(S_k\) is intended to denote the set of exponent residue classes
modulo the relevant suffix period for which the final \(k\) digits
avoid \(\forbidden\).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Status.}
This formula is recorded here as a conjectural counting statement,
not as a theorem. Its validity depends on a precise definition of
the exponent period, a proof that the suffix classification is
complete, and a rigorous derivation of the claimed recurrence.
Those steps have not yet been established in this note.

In particular, a lifting argument from suffix length \(k\) to
\(k+1\) must account for the parity of the new digit. A claim that
every admissible class has exactly three admissible lifts cannot be
assumed merely from the fact that five possible lifts occur: the
number of allowed digits depends on whether the new digit is odd
or even.

\section{Computational Evidence}

Finite computation can search for counterexamples to the uniqueness
conjecture. It cannot, by itself, prove that no counterexample exists
at a larger exponent.

The following Python program checks every exponent in a specified
finite range using exact integer arithmetic:

\begin{verbatim}
FORBIDDEN = set("1248")

def avoids_forbidden_digits(n):
    return all(digit not in FORBIDDEN for digit in str(2**n))

limit = 100_000

solutions = [
    (n, 2**n)
    for n in range(limit + 1)
    if avoids_forbidden_digits(n)
]

for exponent, value in solutions:
    print(exponent, value)
\end{verbatim}

This computation is finite and reproducible. It tests every exponent
from \(0\) through \(100{,}000\), inclusive. A report of a search to
a larger bound should be accompanied by the actual code, execution
environment, and output before being treated as independently
verified evidence.

\begin{remark}
A finite search can establish the statement
\[
P(n)\implies n=16
\quad\text{for }0\leq n\leq N
\]
for its tested bound \(N\). It does not establish the corresponding
statement for all \(n\geq0\).
\end{remark}

\section{Further Questions}

The preceding elementary restrictions leave infinitely many candidate
exponents. A complete proof of the uniqueness conjecture would need
an argument that controls all decimal digit positions, rather than
only a fixed number of final digits.

The modular approach suggests several questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can the admissible suffix classes be described by an exact
    recurrence as the suffix length increases?
    \item Can the number of admissible classes be bounded sharply
    enough to yield useful information about the exponents \(n\)?
    \item Can any such counting argument establish uniqueness, or
    does it only show that admissible exponents are sparse?
    \item Is there a connection with established results on the
    distribution of digits in sequences such as \(2^n\)?
\end{enumerate}

A density estimate, even if proved, would not alone establish
uniqueness. A set can have density zero and still contain infinitely
many elements. The central challenge remains to exclude every
exponent other than \(16\).

\section{Conclusion}

The identity
\[
2^{16}=65536
\]
provides a simple example of an unusual decimal digit pattern.
Elementary modular arguments show that any positive solution must
have exponent divisible by four, and a two-digit analysis eliminates
one quarter of the resulting exponent classes modulo twenty.

These observations do not prove the uniqueness conjecture. The
residue-class counting approach is a possible direction for further
work, but its proposed recurrence and asymptotic consequences require
a complete proof before they can be stated as established results.
